# Bleach bypass: leaving the silver in

In a normal color process, the bleach turns all the developed silver back into halide and the fixer washes it away, leaving only the dyes. Skip the bleach, or weaken it, and a black-and-white silver image stays on top of the color one. Kodak puts it simply: the retained silver increases contrast and decreases color saturation.

The cinematographer Kazuo Miyagawa, who had trained as a chemist, invented it for Kon Ichikawa's Her Brother in 1960. Labs later made it controllable: Technicolor's ENR, named for Ernesto Novelli Rimo, who devised it for Vittorio Storaro on Reds, and Deluxe's CCE, which left about three quarters of the silver in the prints of Seven. Saving Private Ryan used one of the strongest ENR levels Technicolor had run, to drain color from faces.

Done to the negative, as here, the silver sits where the negative is dense: in the scene's highlights. The lab's scan, set for a bleached negative, reads it as extra exposure, so highlights crowd together and colors fade. Labs advise underexposing about a stop. The silver can be bleached later, so a bypassed negative is not lost.
