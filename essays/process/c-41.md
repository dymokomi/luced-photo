# C-41: the color negative process for still film

C-41 is the color negative process of still photography, introduced by Kodak in 1972 together with Kodacolor II. It runs at 37.8 °C (100 °F) with a developer time of three minutes fifteen seconds, and every minilab in the world could run it, which made color negative film the film of family snapshots for decades.

Its developing agent, CD-4, is more active than ECN-2's. Still films are made for it, and need no rem-jet removal. Motion picture film run through C-41, as some labs do, comes out denser and more contrasty than in ECN-2, and its rem-jet has to be dealt with.

Kodak's push rule for C-41 is thirty seconds more per stop: 3:45 for one stop, 4:15 for two. A push raises contrast, the blue layer's most, and grain, but adds little real shadow speed: the shadows of an underexposed negative stay thin.
