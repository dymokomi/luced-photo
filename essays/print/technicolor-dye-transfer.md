# Technicolor dye transfer

Technicolor's imbibition process made prints not by exposing a color film but by printing with dye, like a lithograph. Three black-and-white matrices, one for each color record, were made with a gelatin relief whose thickness followed the image; each was soaked in its dye, yellow, magenta or cyan, and pressed in register onto a blank film, which absorbed the dye. The print was dye alone: no couplers, no mask, no print grain, and dyes so stable that the prints have not faded.

Dye transfer prints are known for dense blacks, saturated color and contrast; the 1990s literature gives their densities as reaching 5.0, against about 3.8 for color print film. The process ended in the United States in the mid-1970s; The Godfather Part II was the last American film printed this way, until a brief revival for films such as Toy Story 2 around 1998–2002. The Godfather's prints were struck from the cut camera negative itself.

Technicolor never published its dyes. Here they come from a 2018 measurement of a 1949 print by Flueckiger and colleagues (yellow at 460 nm, magenta at 540 with a second peak at 575, cyan at 660 and 720), and the tone scale is an estimate: Vision 2383's shape at a tenth more density, grey balanced as a visual neutral.
