# Development: making the latent image visible

The developer is a reducing agent. It turns exposed grains, those carrying a latent image, into metallic silver, and leaves unexposed grains alone, at least for a while. Given enough time, it starts on them too, which shows as fog.

How far development goes depends on time, temperature and agitation. Longer, warmer or more agitated development produces more density, mostly in the highlights, so contrast rises. It also lets weakly exposed grains develop, which effectively raises the film's speed. That is a push: rate the film faster, underexpose, and develop longer. A pull does the opposite.

In color development, the developer that has given up its electrons reacts with the couplers next to the grain and forms dye. The silver is later bleached away, leaving only the dye clouds, which is why color grain looks soft and round.

Here, time and temperature start at the process's own normal, from the manufacturer's tables.

Development is not instant and not even. Silver grows toward what the exposure made developable, quickly at first and slowing as it nears its end; a grain that caught more light develops sooner. As it goes, the developer is used up and bromide and development inhibitors are released into the gelatin. They spread tens of micrometres, so a dense area holds back development next to it, and a thin area next to a dense one gets fresh developer to spare: lines are drawn sharper, borders denser. This is the Eberhard effect, and it is chemistry, not optics. Agitation decides how fast fresh developer arrives. A machine's continuous flow washes the differences out; a tank inverted every half minute leaves some; stand development, left alone for an hour, leaves the most. That is why stand development is loved for acutance and dreaded for its streaks. With Watch Development on (Photo menu, Shift-D), the film develops again after each change, the image building up as it would in the tank.
