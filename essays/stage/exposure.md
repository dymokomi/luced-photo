# Exposure: how much light reaches the film

Exposure is light times time: how bright the image on the film plane is, and for how long the shutter lets it fall. Photographers count it in stops. One stop is a doubling or a halving.

Negative film is forgiving. A color negative records about ten stops or more, and two stops of overexposure often look better than a normal exposure, with denser, cleaner shadows. Underexposure is what hurts: shadow detail never reaches the film, and the grain of a thin negative shows.

Slide film is the opposite. Its range is about five or six stops, and highlights burn out with little warning. Slide shooters often underexposed by a third of a stop to keep color deep.

Here, Exposure changes the light reaching the film, and nothing later takes it back. Underexpose and then push in the Develop node, and you see what a pushed negative really looks like.

Shutter is how long that light fell: the photo's own time until you move it. For most exposures it changes nothing, since exposure is light times time. Past about a second, film stops keeping that bargain. A latent image starts as a few silver atoms, and in dim light the first ones fall apart before the next photon arrives, so the film needs more light than the meter says. This is reciprocity failure. Tri-X wants 50 seconds where the meter says 10. Each layer of a color film fails at its own rate, which is why Kodak asks for a red filter on Vision3 500T at ten seconds and Fujifilm a magenta one on Velvia at half a minute. Dark parts of the picture fail more than bright ones, so long exposures come out contrastier as well as darker.
