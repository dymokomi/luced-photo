# The scan: reading the negative back as light

A negative is not a picture yet. It is inverted, and color negatives carry an orange mask, extra dye that corrects for the cyan and magenta dyes absorbing light they should not. To see the image, the film is read as densities and turned back into light.

For television, that was the telecine: a Rank Cintel flying-spot scanner, and later the Spirit DataCine, reading the negative straight to video. A colorist set the transfer per scene: how warm, how dense, where black sits. Modern film scanners read printing density, the densities print film would see, so a scan can stand in for a print.

The scan here measures the negative through the densities its dyes produce and maps them back to scene light, set up once for a normally exposed and developed negative. Like a telecine, it is calibrated on a reference negative, Kodak's Vision3 250D, so each stock shows its own color: the softer palette of 1990s EXR, the saturation of Vision3 50D or Ektar. Calibration set to Matched fits the scanner to the stock instead, returning colors to themselves. Timing (Warmth and Tint), Contrast and Black adjust it as the telecine operator would.
