# Film: an emulsion of silver halide

Film is a thin plastic base coated with gelatin holding crystals of silver halide, the grains. A photon absorbed by a grain can leave a few atoms of metallic silver, a latent image too small to see. Bigger grains catch light more easily, so fast films have bigger grains and show more grain.

Color film stacks three such layers, made sensitive by dyes to blue, green and red light, each holding a coupler that will become a dye: yellow, magenta and cyan. Between and within the layers, chemistry links them. Development in one layer releases inhibitors that hold back its neighbors, the interimage effect, which makes film colors richer than a simple mix.

Light also scatters inside the emulsion, and some goes on into the base and reflects off its back: halation. Beyond a critical angle the base's back surface reflects all of it, so the light returns as a ring about twice the base's thickness away, roughly a quarter of a millimetre on 35 mm film, then fainter rings beyond. Most light that gets that far is red, and on its way back it meets the red-sensitive layer first, so halos glow red-orange. Makers stop it with a backing: rem-jet, a carbon-black layer on motion picture negatives that the lab washes off; an antihalation layer that clears in processing; or a grey-dyed base. CineStill sells Kodak's Vision3 with the rem-jet taken off, and its halos are the whole point. Backing: Removed shows what that does to any stock.
